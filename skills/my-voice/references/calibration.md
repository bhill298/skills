# Calibration and evidence

## What the evidence supports

This profile comes from selected messages, sender-filtered email passages, technical requests, and notes. It is not an exhaustive survey. Quoted replies, signatures, code, copied answers, automatic prompts, and obvious memes are not evidence of the user's prose. Notes contain mixed material and support a trait most strongly when it also appears in original correspondence.

Well supported across the reviewed correspondence:

- Practical reasons and concrete conditions accompany requests.
- Preferences remain distinct from requirements or acceptable fallbacks.
- Qualifications can arrive after an initial claim rather than being integrated into a polished sentence.
- Repetition sometimes narrows a condition or makes a preference clearer.
- Conversational wording carries fairly dense explanations.
- Courtesy, enthusiasm, and irritation vary with the exchange; directness is compatible with all three.

Supported within particular contexts: fragments and omitted final periods in chat; chronological attempts and clipped actions in troubleshooting notes; more conventional capitalization in email. These are register choices, not features to spread across every draft.

Less established: exact slang frequencies, punctuation rates, humor across audiences, and a characteristic voice for essays, announcements, or sensitive interpersonal boundaries. Do not treat a plausible synthetic example as evidence for these. Older formal emails show an available register, while older chat includes era-specific language. Recent, comparable writing should lead unless the task requests otherwise.

## Reading the examples

Examples labeled **adapted** retain an observed way of constructing a thought while changing its subject and circumstances. They are not verbatim quotations. Examples labeled **synthetic** demonstrate a supported habit in an invented situation; they do not establish a new trait. Neither type supplies facts for a draft.

Select an example by conversational purpose and relationship, not just by whether the destination is chat or email. A brief answer and a detailed explanation should not converge on the same length or polish.

## Brief synthetic examples

### Scheduling reply

Brief: A friend proposes Saturday at 3. It works, but you must leave at 5.

> yeah 3 works. I'll have to leave around 5 though

Normal capitalization is also plausible. No reason or extra question is needed unless the brief supplies one.

### Service email

Brief: You ordered blue, but the confirmation says gray. Ask for a correction before shipping.

> Hello,
>
> I ordered the blue version, but the confirmation email says gray. Can you change it to blue before it ships?
>
> Thanks

The contrast reports a discrepancy. The example does not imply that every complaint needs a greeting, explicit request, or sign-off.

### Private note

Brief: A display problem occurs after waking. Reconnecting the cable fixes it. Another port is untested.

> display issue after wake
> - reconnecting cable fixes it
> - try another port (haven't tested yet)

The clipped wording suits a note; it would be abrupt in an email asking for help.
