# Write in my voice

Apply the profile to the requested draft or edit, not to ordinary assistant replies or unrelated future requests. Return only the requested text unless the user asks for commentary or alternatives.

The voice is conversational and specific. Requests often include the practical reason, a qualification, or an acceptable alternative. Dense reasoning can use ordinary language. The amount of explanation varies sharply with the situation.

## Choose the right evidence

The user's requested tone and a current writing sample take precedence. Otherwise, favor recent writing for a similar audience and purpose; use older or less comparable samples to fill gaps. Do not average formal correspondence, gaming chat, and instructions to an agent into one voice.

The strongest evidence is for everyday messages, practical emails, and technical explanations. Notes support a rougher register. Essays, public announcements, and sensitive personal letters are less established: carry over supported habits conservatively rather than inventing a distinctive voice for them. See [references/calibration.md](references/calibration.md) for evidence strength and brief examples.

Use the supplied facts, attitudes, and degree of commitment. Do not invent experiences, feelings, relationships, expertise, promises, or certainty to make the voice convincing. Examples illustrate phrasing; their circumstances are not facts to reuse.

## How the thoughts develop

- Start with the answer, situation, request, or concrete problem. Context can come first when the reader needs it. "I was wondering if..." fits a favor or feasibility question; directness does not require an imperative.
- Explain the practical reason when it matters: the inconvenience, failed workaround, uncertainty, or consequence of changing something. Keep conditions that make the request specific, even when they need more space than the request itself.
- Distinguish acceptable from preferred. Something can be fine as it is while a change would be nicer. Explain the limit on an alternative when supplied, without adding concessions to a firm requirement.
- State an observation directly and qualify its interpretation separately. "I think," "seems like," "probably," and "I'm not sure" belong near the actual uncertainty. Preserve small words that set scope: "one downside" does not mean "the main downside" or "the only downside." Smoother phrasing should not make a claim stronger or more exhaustive.
- Let a qualification arrive after the claim: "Or at least I couldn't find it." A sentence can also end in "though" or "if I remember right." Do not automatically move these to the front or turn them into balanced clauses.
- Preserve useful restatement. The user may repeat a preference, concrete term, or condition to clarify its exact scope. Remove accidental duplication without tightening everything to the fewest possible words.

These are available moves, not a message outline. An acknowledgment may need none of them. A complex explanation can use several without becoming a formal report.

## Rhythm and wording

Short confirmations coexist with long sentences that accumulate a reason, condition, and parenthetical. Some thoughts arrive as an addition: "Also," "But," "Anyway," or "I'll add that..." Keep that progression when it is easy to follow. Clarify genuinely confusing syntax without reorganizing every paragraph into an ideal outline.

Use ordinary connectors and familiar words, including technical terms the reader knows. Contractions, "a bit," "pretty," "just," "actually," and "for now" can serve a real purpose. They are options, not verbal signatures. Repeat the right noun instead of rotating through elaborate synonyms.

Prefer periods, commas, and parentheses. Colons suit concrete explanations and lists. Slashes can group closely related alternatives, especially in notes and practical messages. Em dashes should not become the default connector. Preserve clear conversational looseness when editing; do not manufacture errors, tense changes, or awkwardness in a new draft.

Warmth and irritation should match the supplied attitude. Specific thanks and uncomplicated enthusiasm fit, including an exclamation point when warranted. Annoyance can be plain and factual. Avoid turning either into elaborate praise, an apology, or an accusation the user did not express.

## Match the situation

### Casual chat

Use contractions, fragments, and short replies where appropriate. Capitalization is mixed; lowercase starts and missing final periods can fit quick messages, while longer explanations often use normal sentences. Do not lowercase names or technical identifiers indiscriminately.

A reply can assume shared context and add just one thought. Preserve separate messages when editing an exchange; for a single requested reply, use natural paragraph breaks rather than inventing multiple sends. Humor can rely on the situation or shared context. Older memes, emphatic capitals, emoticons, and profanity are not a default recipe for current writing.

For late additions, loose preferences, tentative requests, and appreciation, read [references/conversation-examples.md](references/conversation-examples.md).

### Email

Use normal capitalization and generally complete sentences, with room for an afterthought or fragment in a familiar exchange. A new exchange can use a short greeting and thanks. An ongoing thread can begin with the answer and end when the point is made. Include a signature only when appropriate, using a name supplied for the task.

Ordinary courtesy need not become ceremonial politeness. A discrepancy can be certain while its meaning remains unclear. Preserve that distinction in a tentative opening rather than automatically making the complaint blunter. Conversely, an established problem need not acquire "I might be missing something."

A repeat complaint can start with what is still happening and name the inconvenience. In a short follow-up, let the recurring problem and lack of response carry the irritation. Treat a tone label in a drafting brief as guidance for delivery, not another sentence to paraphrase as "I'm frustrated" or "I'm annoyed." Preserve explicit emotional wording in an existing draft or when the user asks to state the feeling. Do not automatically append a request for confirmation, an apology, or a summary. Once resolved, a brief acknowledgment may be enough. See the terse follow-up in [references/conversation-examples.md](references/conversation-examples.md).

### Technical requests and explanations

Concrete behavior, exact labels, conditions, and exceptions matter more than elegant compression. Include the outcome, observed behavior, attempted fixes, and remaining uncertainty when supplied. Put an exception near the behavior it changes.

Bullets suit separate issues or acceptance conditions; connected prose suits the reasoning behind a choice. A long explanation may move from an observation to a tentative cause, a workaround, and why that workaround is insufficient. Do not impose a bug-report template on an ordinary message or append stock testing and maintenance demands.

For sustained reasoning and qualifications, read [references/extended-examples.md](references/extended-examples.md).

### Notes to self

Fragments, quantities, compact bullets, small labels, arrows, and parenthetical caveats are natural. Separate observations from hypotheses and next actions. Omit setup intended for an outside reader.

Longer troubleshooting notes can preserve the sequence of attempts, false starts, and discoveries. Full sentences can alternate with clipped actions. Keep the evidence that changed the interpretation rather than rewriting the account as if the answer were obvious from the start. The final line may simply be an outstanding action. See the chronological example in [references/extended-examples.md](references/extended-examples.md).

## Final style pass

Check meaning first: retain the supplied details, uncertainty, emotional strength, preferences, and limits. Then check for staging or excess polish:

- Cut announcements of the point, inflated significance, and closers that merely repeat it.
- Remove invented opposing positions and rhetorical question-and-answer routines. Keep real contrasts, questions, jokes, and objections that belong to the exchange.
- Replace corporate or abstract phrasing with the actual action or consequence. Do not substitute staged casualness such as "real talk."
- Remove forced parallel rhythms and presentation formatting unsuited to the destination. Keep real lists, useful headings, and meaningful repetition.
- Check whether editing erased a late qualification, conversational addition, or explanation of why a workaround fails. Restore it when supported by the input.

Specific evidence of this voice takes precedence over generic AI-tell removal. Naturalness comes from the purpose, details, and relationship; adding slang or mistakes will not repair a generic draft. This pass is self-contained and does not require another skill.
